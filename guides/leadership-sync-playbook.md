# Leadership Sync Playbook

A public-safe guide for preparing a work conversation when a project needs clearer ownership, time commitment, and delivery rhythm.

This guide intentionally avoids company names, project names, people names, private metrics, and internal details.

## When to Use This

Use this when:

- a teammate's ownership is unclear;
- a project has delivery pressure or staffing constraints;
- a manager or stakeholder expects clearer execution rhythm;
- a teammate may be frustrated or misaligned;
- you need to clarify what someone will own, support, or escalate.

## Core Frame

Do not frame the conversation as personal pressure.

Frame it as:

> The project needs clearer ownership, time commitment, and risk visibility so the team can make a realistic delivery plan.

The goal is not to make the other person immediately happy. The goal is to leave with a clear working agreement.

## Common Failure Modes

### Too Soft

Symptoms:

- Avoiding the hard topic.
- Leaving without a concrete commitment.
- Accepting vague availability without discussing delivery risk.
- Trying to keep the relationship comfortable at the cost of project clarity.

### Too Hard

Symptoms:

- Turning stakeholder expectations into personal pressure.
- Sounding like the decision is unilateral.
- Ignoring the teammate's constraints.
- Creating defensiveness instead of ownership.

## Recommended Conversation Structure

### 1. Align on Context

Say what has changed and why the conversation matters.

Example:

> I want to sync on ownership and execution rhythm for this project. The delivery window is getting tighter, and we need a more explicit plan for who owns what and where the risks are.

Cover:

- current project pressure;
- why ownership needs to be clearer;
- what stakeholders are concerned about;
- why vague availability creates delivery risk.

### 2. Acknowledge Constraints

Show that you heard the other person's constraints before asking for clarity.

Example:

> I understand you have other priorities and may not be able to spend all your time here. The part we need to clarify is what scope is realistic under that constraint.

Cover:

- competing priorities;
- limited availability;
- current workload;
- areas where they may only be able to support rather than own.

### 3. Convert Ambiguity Into Options

Do not ask a vague question like “Can you help more?”

Ask for concrete ownership choices:

- Which tasks can you own end to end?
- Which tasks can you support but not own?
- Which tasks should move to someone else?
- What risks should we escalate early?
- What would make this plan realistic for you?

### 4. Close With a Working Agreement

End with explicit next steps.

Capture:

- owner;
- scope;
- expected time rhythm;
- next checkpoint;
- escalation path if the plan is not realistic.

Example:

> To make sure I understood: you will own X, support Y, and flag by Thursday if Z is at risk. I will update the project plan and bring up staffing or scope risk if needed.

## Five-line Prep Template

Before the sync, write this down:

```text
1. Context: What project pressure or constraint makes this sync necessary?
2. Desired outcome: What must be clear by the end of the conversation?
3. Their constraints: What do I already know about their priorities or availability?
4. Ownership options: What can they own, support, or hand off?
5. Close: What next step, checkpoint, or escalation path do I need?
```

## Daily Work Log Entry After the Sync

After the conversation, record:

- what was clarified;
- what ownership changed;
- what risk was surfaced;
- what decision or next checkpoint was created;
- what could be reused later as leadership evidence.

## Brag-doc Seed Pattern

A useful sanitized brag-doc bullet might look like:

> Clarified ownership and execution rhythm for a constrained project by turning ambiguous availability into explicit scope, support responsibilities, risk visibility, and follow-up checkpoints.

## Privacy Check

Before saving notes from a real conversation, remove:

- real names;
- internal project names;
- private dates or launch details;
- confidential staffing details;
- restricted links;
- employee-sensitive information;
- customer or partner references.
